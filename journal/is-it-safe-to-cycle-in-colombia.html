Is It Safe to Cycle in Colombia? An Honest Local Answer

The question we get asked most. Here is the direct answer — including the risks that are real and the ones that are thirty years out of date.

By Sergio · Founder & Lead Guide Boyacá, Colombia 8 min read Published August 2026
Alta montaña boyacense

This is the question we are asked most often, usually in a slightly apologetic tone, and it deserves a direct answer rather than a marketing one. Yes, we think cycling in rural Boyacá is safe — and the reasons people usually worry about are not the reasons that actually matter here.

What follows is the honest version: what has changed, what genuinely requires care, and what we do about each of them.

01 — The reputationWhat is genuinely out of date

Most international anxiety about Colombia is anchored in the country of the late 1980s and 1990s. That country has changed substantially. Colombia today receives millions of international visitors a year, and cycling tourism in particular has grown because riders keep coming back and telling other riders.

Boyacá specifically is one of the calmer departments in the country — agricultural, conservative in the small-town sense, and genuinely proud of cycling. A group of visiting cyclists here is not an oddity; it is a familiar sight in a region that hosts a two-thousand-rider gran fondo every year.

None of that means "nowhere in Colombia requires thought". It means the specific corridor we operate in is not the place the old reputation was built on, and that the honest risks are ordinary ones.

02 — The real risksWhat we actually plan around

In ten years of riding these roads, the hazards that have mattered are not dramatic:

  • Traffic behaviour. Overtaking habits on rural Colombian roads are more assertive than in northern Europe. Trucks and buses give less room than you are used to.
  • Road surface. Patched tarmac, gravel washed into corners after rain, and the occasional unmarked hazard. Descending here demands more attention than descending in the Alps.
  • Weather at altitude. A páramo afternoon can turn cold and wet quickly. Hypothermia on a long descent is a more realistic concern than anything else on this list.
  • Dogs. Every rural cycling region has them. Boyacá is no exception.
  • Altitude. Genuinely the most common thing that affects our riders — which is why we wrote a separate guide about it.
  • Opportunistic theft in cities. Standard big-city caution in Bogotá: do not leave a bike unattended, do not display expensive equipment on the street.

The honest answer is that the risks here are the risks of cycling anywhere in the mountains — plus altitude, minus the traffic density of Europe.

03 — What we do about itThe convoy, and why it is shaped that way

Every stage runs with the same configuration: a bilingual lead guide and a local rider-mechanic riding with the group, a lead motorcycle ahead positioning the photographer and signalling the route, and a support van at the rear.

That rear van is the safety backbone. It carries water, spares and layers, it means a mechanical does not strand anybody, and it means a rider having a bad altitude day has a dignified option. The lead moto matters for a different reason: on a road where overtaking is assertive, having a vehicle ahead of the group changes how drivers approach.

  • We do not ride at night, and we do not ride routes we have not reconnoitred that season.
  • Routes are chosen for surface and traffic volume, not just for scenery — several beautiful roads in Boyacá are not on our itinerary for exactly this reason.
  • Helmets are required at all times on the bike, without exception.
  • The guides are local. They know which descents hold gravel, and they read weather off the páramo before it arrives.
  • Medical and accident assistance is included for the ten days of the tour within Colombia.

04 — What you should doThe rider’s half

  • Bring your own travel insurance. Ours covers medical assistance during the tour; it does not cover trip cancellation or your flights. Check that your policy does not exclude cycling as a hazardous activity — many standard policies do.
  • Ride predictably in the group. Most incidents in any group ride come from within the group, not from outside it.
  • Descend within yourself. The climbs here reward ambition. The descents do not.
  • Tell us about medical conditions. Before the trip, in writing, even if you think it is minor. It changes how we plan, not whether you come.
  • Use the van when you need it. It is there to be used.

05 — The straight answerWould we bring our own family?

We live here. Our lead guide has ridden a bicycle into all 123 municipalities of this department, and directs a two-thousand-rider event on these roads every year. The honest test of a safety claim is whether the people making it accept the same risk themselves, and we do — every week.

If you are still uncertain, the most useful thing you can do is ask us specifics rather than generalities. Which roads, which sections, what happens if it rains, what happens if someone crashes. We would rather answer twenty pointed questions than have you arrive with a vague worry you never voiced.
