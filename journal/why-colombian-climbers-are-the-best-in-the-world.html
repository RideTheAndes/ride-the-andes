Why Colombia Produces the World’s Best Climbers

Altitude is the answer everyone gives. It explains half of it. The other half is geography, economics and a hundred years of the bicycle mattering here.

By Sergio · Founder & Lead Guide Boyacá, Colombia 8 min read Published August 2026
Alto del Cogollo · Gran Fondo Boyacá Mundial

Ask why Colombia produces so many exceptional climbers and you will usually get one answer: altitude. It is the right answer to the wrong question. Altitude explains why Colombian riders can climb. It does not explain why so many of them come from a handful of villages within a few hours of each other.

The full explanation is geographic, economic and cultural at the same time — and you can read most of it from the saddle in a single day in Boyacá.

01 — The physiologyWhat growing up at 2,800 metres does

Children in these villages grow up, go to school and play at altitudes that visiting professionals reach only in training camps. Lifelong exposure to reduced oxygen produces adaptations that a three-week camp cannot: greater oxygen-carrying capacity, more efficient breathing mechanics, a cardiovascular system built from childhood for this air.

Then there is body composition. Climbing rewards a high power-to-weight ratio, and the physiques these mountains produce tend to arrive at that ratio naturally. When a rider raised at 2,800 metres descends to race in Europe, they get an oxygen surplus that their competitors simply do not have.

02 — The terrainThere is no flat road to train on

This is the part that visitors underestimate until they ride here. In Boyacá, there is no flat option. The road out of your village climbs. The road to the next town climbs. A child riding to school is doing what a European junior would call a training session.

The gradients are also long. These are not the short, sharp bergs of the northern classics — they are sustained Andean ascents where the climb is measured in tens of kilometres, and where you learn to manage an effort over an hour rather than attack it over ten minutes. That is precisely the skill a Grand Tour mountain stage demands.

A Boyacá kid does not go out to train on the climbs. The climbs are simply where everything is.

03 — The cultureWhy the bicycle, specifically

Plenty of mountainous countries have altitude and gradients. What Boyacá adds is a hundred years of the bicycle being taken seriously — as transport, as work, and as a way out. The Vuelta a Colombia has been a national event since the middle of the last century, and here the local escarabajos were folk heroes long before European contracts existed.

That matters more than it sounds. A young rider here grows up seeing the bike as a legitimate future rather than an eccentric hobby. There are club structures, local races most weekends, and older riders who will drag a promising teenager around until they can hold a wheel. It is an apprenticeship system built out of enthusiasm rather than budget.

04 — The namesWho actually came from where

Boyacá's own list is genuinely remarkable for a department of this size. Nairo Quintana is from Cómbita. Miguel Ángel "Supermán" López and the late Mauricio Soler are Boyacá-born too. Ride the department and you pass through the towns they came from — often on the same roads they trained on.

The broader altiplano cundiboyacense, the high plateau that Boyacá shares with Cundinamarca, extends the list: Egan Bernal from Zipaquirá, Lucho Herrera from Fusagasugá, Daniel Felipe Martínez from the Bogotá savanna. Different departments, the same air, largely the same kind of roads.

It is worth being precise about this, because Colombian cycling is often flattened into a single origin story. Rigoberto Urán, for instance, is from Urrao in Antioquia — a different region, a different set of mountains, and part of why Colombian cycling is deeper than any one department's contribution.

05 — The proofDuitama, 1995

If you want a single date, take the 1995 Road World Championships, held in Duitama. Abraham Olano won. Miguel Indurain finished second. Marco Pantani was third. The best riders in the world came to Boyacá and raced on these roads — and the local memory of that week has not faded in thirty years.

The closing climb of every edition of the Gran Fondo Boyacá Mundial is the Alto del Cogollo, and part of what makes it a serious finale is that everyone riding it knows who has climbed it before them.
