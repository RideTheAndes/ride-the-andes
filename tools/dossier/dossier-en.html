The Invitation

This is not a tour.
It's a homecoming.

There are one hundred and twenty-three municipalities in Boyacá, and I have ridden a bicycle into every single one of them. Not for a record — because I needed to know, kilometre by kilometre, what my land was made of.

What I found was a territory that produces climbers the way other places produce wheat: children training at three thousand metres, gradients that give nothing for free, villages where a national champion was born on the main street. And between the climbs — clay-pot towns, sacred lakes, colonial plazas, and a warmth that turns a hard day on the bike into something closer to family.

Ride The Andes exists to give that Boyacá back to the world the way it deserves: from the saddle, at altitude, earned. This dossier is the journey we built for it — ten days from the lagoon where the legend of El Dorado was born to the roads of the Gran Fondo Boyacá Mundial, the 2,000-rider event I have the honour of directing shoulder to shoulder with my father — ten editions and counting.

You won't visit Boyacá. You'll be let into it — by the people who are Boyacá.

Ten places per departure. Four windows in 2027, with January as our main one — and always the same small group, riding as one.

The Territory

Why Boyacá

Colombia produces climbers, and Boyacá produces Colombia's climbers.

Nairo Quintana, Miguel Ángel “Supermán” López, Mauricio Soler — all born in Boyacá — and, across the wider Cundiboyacense high plateau, Lucho Herrera, Egan Bernal and Daniel Felipe Martínez. A disproportionate share of the country's cycling nobility learned to suffer on these roads. It isn't mysticism. It's altitude: an entire high country living, working and racing between 2,500 and 3,500 metres, where children ride to school up gradients that would end most amateurs' weekends.

The rest is what nobody tells you. Boyacá is where Colombia became a country — the Puente de Boyacá, the Pantano de Vargas. It holds the continent's largest cobbled plaza, an ancient seabed turned desert, and the highest great lake in the country with a white-sand beach at 3,000 metres.

Ninety minutes from Bogotá, and almost entirely absent from international cycling itineraries.

The Finale

Why the Gran Fondo Boyacá Mundial

Most cycling holidays end with a ride. Yours ends on a legendary course.

The Gran Fondo Boyacá Mundial is the biggest mass-participation road event in Boyacá: more than 2,000 riders rolling out of Duitama on closed roads, through the department that raised half the Colombian peloton. It has run ten editions since 2016 — and it is directed by your guide.

Its course is not incidental. It climbs the Alto del Cogollo, the ascent used for the 1995 UCI Road World Championships, when Indurain, Pantani and Olano raced past front doors in Duitama. Local riders have never stopped measuring themselves on it.

Your final stage rides that course with the man who directs it — every climb, every village and the same finish in Duitama, on open roads and at your own pace. On the October departure it is the real thing: an official start, a real result, and 2,000 Colombians who will drag you up climbs, shout at you in Spanish, and hand you an aguapanela at the top. Either way you finish something that existed long before you arrived and will continue long after.

Before You Ride

The practical answers.

How hard is it, honestly?

A genuine cycling tour for riders comfortable with multi-hour days and sustained climbing — roughly the fitness to ride 3–4 hours on consecutive days. You never need to race: the van is always behind you, and any stage can become a partial stage whenever you choose.

What about the altitude?

The route is sequenced to acclimatise you: lower valley stages first, the high country around Lago de Tota later, with a rest day exactly where it counts. Hydration and pacing are carefully managed; the crew watches the group closely.

My bike, or yours?

Both work. Bring your own bike — we receive it in Bogotá, build it, maintain it daily and pack it for your flight home. Or rent a professionally fitted premium carbon road or all-road bike from our own fleet (not included; reserved in advance).

January weather?

Boyacá's dry season: crisp mornings around 8–12°C, riding hours of 15–22°C and strong sun at altitude — late January brings Paipa about a third of October's rain days, though Lago de Tota stays changeable. Layers, leg warmers and a light shell; a full packing list comes with your confirmation.

Is the race part of it?

The race is in October, and it closes our 9–18 October departure with an official number. In January, May and August, Stage 6 rides the same course with its director — full fondo (143.6 km / +2,120 m) or Medio Fondo (103.2 km / +1,403 m), crew on course.

Who is this NOT for?

Honestly: riders looking for their first-ever cycling holiday, or for flat spins between cafés. This is real climbing at real altitude — earned, supported, and unforgettable. If you're in doubt, write us; we'll tell you straight.

What does the cover include?

Medical and accident assistance for all ten days in Colombia, with road cycling expressly listed — not excluded as a hazardous sport, as many standard policies do. Full policy details come with your confirmation. It doesn't cover trip cancellation, so we recommend adding your own travel insurance for that.

Can I cancel?

Yes. Within 5 business days of booking, you get everything back — no deductions, no questions. After that a published scale applies, and at any point you can hand your place to another rider at no cost. Full terms live at ridetheandes.co.

Arrive: Friday, January 22 — any hour; we meet every flight at El Dorado (BOG).   Depart: Sunday, January 31 — we reach the airport at 2:00 pm, so book flights leaving after 5:00 pm.
